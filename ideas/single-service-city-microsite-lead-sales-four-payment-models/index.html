Single-service, Single-city Lead-gen Microsites Monetized Four Ways (exclusive Lead Fee, Rental, Revenue Share, or Brokering the Job)

Marketing & content · Near-zero cost

Originally published Sep 15, 2026 — Jesse Cunningham

Share on Facebook Link copied
Source

Jesse Cunningham · How to Create an AI Microsite Business in 3 Easy Steps ↗

What It Is

You use an AI assistant to build a small flat-HTML website that targets one urgent, high-ticket service in one city (for example sewer line replacement in Dallas or custom pool builders in Orlando), with roughly 40-50 pages covering the service, each surrounding town, pricing, and the questions people search when the problem hits. Once it ranks and the phone rings, you hand the calls to a local contractor. The contractor pays you, either per lead, as monthly rent for the site, as a percentage of the job, or you act as the go-between on the job yourself.

The Pitch

Cunningham argues that a narrowly focused site is easy for Google to understand, so it ranks more readily than a general company site with many services and a blog. He says a site built this way costs him about $11 (just the domain) and about 15 minutes with his AI blueprint, and that even nine calls a month is valuable because a contractor who closes three $8,000 jobs gets more than $20,000 of work. He lays out four ways to get paid: an exclusive flat fee of about $150 per lead (which he says contractors prefer to resold leads from Angie's List-type services), renting the whole site for $500-$1,000 a month, a revenue share such as 5% of a $150,000 pool job (his favorite), or working the lead yourself and sourcing the contractor at a markup where licensing allows. He compares the model to HomeAdvisor, Angie's List, and Zillow as lead providers.

Best For

Patient people comfortable with basic SEO and website building who are willing to call and negotiate with local tradespeople. Suits someone who can wait weeks or months with no results before any income appears.

The Truth

Rank and rent is a long-established, real model, and focusing a site on one service in one city is a reasonable SEO approach; the near-zero build cost is plausible for a static HTML site. What is not shown is any of his own lead volume, contractor deals, or payments: the nine-calls, $150-per-lead, and 5%-rev-share figures are hypothetical examples, and his 'six figures a month' claim is his own, unaudited. He openly says the site sits doing nothing for weeks, most early sites fail, and even experienced builders see many flops; the many-towns-page structure can also look like thin location pages to Google, and finding and trusting a contractor to report revenue for a rev share is a real hurdle. The fourth option of working the lead yourself may require trade licensing and involves going to customer properties. The video promotes his paid SEO academy community (7-day free trial) and its microsite blueprint, which he positions as the key to building these sites.
